**Current best practices in single-cell RNA-seq analysis: a tutorial**

<https://www.embopress.org/doi/full/10.15252/msb.20188746>

The document provides a comprehensive tutorial on best practices for analyzing single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data, outlining standardized approaches from initial preprocessing to advanced downstream analyses. As scRNA-seq technologies expand rapidly, the field faces challenges in maintaining consistency due to the large and growing number of analysis tools-385 as of 2019-primarily built in R and Python. Although integrated platforms like Seurat, Scanpy, and Scater offer streamlined workflows, their language-specific design limits cross-environment flexibility. To address these issues, the tutorial summarizes current best practices based on comparative studies and provides a detailed case study on GitHub for practical guidance.

A typical scRNA-seq workflow begins with raw sequencing reads processed into count matrices, followed by several critical steps including quality control (QC), normalization, data correction, feature selection, and dimensionality reduction. QC is emphasized as essential and relies on metrics such as read depth, number of detected genes, and mitochondrial content; the document recommends evaluating these jointly and applying permissive thresholds to avoid discarding viable cells. Normalization methods such as CPM and scran's pooling-based approach help address variability in sequencing depth, while nonlinear approaches may be more appropriate for plate-based datasets.

Data correction and integration address unwanted technical variation and batch effects. Approaches like regression-based correction for biological or technical covariates, as well as batch correction strategies including ComBat, Harmony, and CCA, are evaluated, with warnings against over-correction that may obscure true biological signals. Expression recovery tools-MAGIC, DCA, and scVI-offer ways to mitigate dropout noise but must be used cautiously due to risks of introducing artificial correlations.

To manage high dimensionality, the workflow recommends selecting 1,000-5,000 highly variable genes and applying dimensionality reduction techniques such as PCA, UMAP, and diffusion maps depending on the analysis goal. Preprocessed data can exist in multiple stages-raw, normalized, corrected, feature-selected, or reduced-each suitable for specific downstream tasks.

Downstream analyses include clustering, trajectory inference, differential expression testing, gene set enrichment, and regulatory network inference. Methods like the Louvain algorithm, Slingshot, MAST, and limma are highlighted for robust performance. The tutorial concludes by emphasizing ongoing challenges, including data sparsity, batch effects, and computational complexity, while highlighting future directions such as deep learning-based pipelines, multi-omic integration, and development of standardized benchmarks to guide the evolving scRNA-seq landscape.
